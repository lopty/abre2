Living in Dubai, Owning Property in India: Get the Paperwork in Order

An Indian property can remain an important part of family life long after its owner moves to Dubai. It may be a future home, a rental property, an inherited asset or a place a parent still lives.

Distance makes small gaps harder to resolve. A missing document becomes a search across several households. A conversation with a tenant becomes a chain of forwarded messages. A proposed sale raises questions nobody needed to answer while the owner was nearby.

The practical starting point is an organised property file. That will not replace legal due diligence, but it makes the next conversation far more useful.

Know which property and documents you are discussing

Collect the ownership records you hold, the property description, earlier transaction documents and any current lease or management instruction. Note which originals are available and who has them.

Do not assume that an administrative record, a payment receipt and an ownership document establish the same thing. Ask an Indian property lawyer to explain what each record shows and what still needs checking for your particular property.

If details differ across documents, record the differences. Avoid quietly editing a description to make the file appear consistent.

Clarify the authority to act

Many overseas owners rely on a relative or representative. That can be helpful, but practical assistance and legal authority are separate questions.

Before someone signs, receives money or deals with a proposed transaction, ask counsel what authority is required and how the relevant organisation will verify it. A power of attorney should be reviewed for the intended task, its acceptance requirements and its limits.

Do not treat a document prepared for one transaction as automatically suitable for a later sale, tenancy or dispute.

Separate maintenance from ownership decisions

Appointing someone to arrange repairs does not answer who can change a tenancy, approve a sale or settle a disagreement. Keep those instructions distinct.

For inherited or jointly owned property, establish whose interests need to be considered before discussing a commercial outcome. The family’s preferred plan and the legal position may require separate conversations.

An owner based in the UAE should also agree how updates, quotations and approvals will be recorded. A short written decision trail is easier to follow than months of scattered messages.

Treat the property file as an ongoing record

A property folder is often assembled only when a sale is being discussed. By then, records may be spread across an old email account, a relative's cupboard and a former representative's phone. An overseas owner can make the process easier by maintaining a simple inventory throughout ownership.

List the documents you have without deciding what each one proves. Add the date, the name appearing on it and whether you hold an original or a copy. If a document is missing, mark it as missing. Ask your adviser which gaps matter for the proposed decision rather than assuming that every available paper has equal significance.

Make jointly owned property a separate conversation

A family may agree that a home should eventually be sold without agreeing on the steps needed to reach that point. Different people can have different expectations about occupation, expenditure, proceeds or timing. Living abroad can make those assumptions harder to spot.

Prepare a factual record of the people named in the documents and the people currently using or managing the home. Keep a separate note of the family's preferred plan. A lawyer can then help explain the questions that need resolving. Avoid presenting a family consensus as a legal conclusion before the relevant ownership and authority records have been reviewed.

Ask for evidence behind a proposed transaction

When someone offers to buy or rent a property, an overseas owner may feel pressure to move quickly because another visit to India is inconvenient. That pressure should not replace a review of the actual proposal. Request the draft terms, the identity of the parties and a clear explanation of the payments.

If the proposed process changes, record the change. A new buyer, a different payee or a revised signing arrangement may require further checks. Verify instructions through a known contact rather than relying on a forwarded message. Ask your adviser what should be confirmed before money or original documents change hands.

Keep a decision log for the people helping you

A relative can be doing their best while still working from incomplete instructions. Give each person a clear role. Someone arranging access for a surveyor does not need to be left guessing whether they may also negotiate a price. Someone forwarding documents should know which copies are appropriate to share.

For significant decisions, keep a short dated record of what was approved and by whom. Include the document version or quotation being discussed. This helps the owner and representative explain the same arrangement later, especially if several family members have been involved at different stages.

Plan the handover between advisers

A property matter may involve a lawyer, an accountant, an institution and a local representative. Ask who needs which information and how the work will be coordinated. If an issue falls outside an adviser's role, make that boundary explicit instead of allowing it to disappear between conversations.

For example, a question about ownership records is different from a question about the physical condition of the building. A discussion of proceeds may require a different professional from the person reviewing a proposed sale agreement. Ask the professionals involved to identify those dependencies early enough for you to make a considered decision.

Leave the next owner or representative a usable file

After a transaction or change of representative, update your records rather than keeping the old arrangement as the default. Record who now holds keys, documents and contact details. Collect receipts and final versions of signed documents, and note any follow-up action that remains outstanding.

For a UAE-based owner, good administration means being able to understand the position without starting again each time. It does not remove the need for professional advice. It gives that advice a clearer foundation and reduces the likelihood that an important decision is made from an incomplete description sent during a busy working day.
