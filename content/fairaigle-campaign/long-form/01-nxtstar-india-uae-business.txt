Your UAE Business Is Open. Are Your India Agreements Ready?

A business licence can mark the beginning of a new chapter in Dubai. It does not settle every question connected to the founder’s business interests in India.

A supplier may still be based in Bengaluru. A development team may work from Hyderabad. A co-founder may remain in Mumbai. Customer payments, intellectual property and family investments can sit on both sides of the relationship.

For an Indian entrepreneur living in the UAE, the challenge is often less about geography than responsibility: who is authorised to do what, under which agreement, and with what record?

Start with the actual relationship

Before collecting templates, describe the arrangement in plain language. Is the Indian business a supplier, a shareholder, a distributor or a separate company under common ownership? Who invoices the customer? Who receives the payment? Who owns the deliverables?

Those answers give advisers something concrete to review. A document called a “partnership agreement” may not describe the arrangement the parties are actually operating.

Make a simple map of the entities, people and payment flows. Include the agreements already signed and the decisions that are still being made informally.

Put important decisions in writing

Consider a UAE founder commissioning software from an Indian team. A friendly working relationship does not answer what happens to unfinished work, customer information or access credentials if the arrangement ends.

The useful questions are practical. What is being delivered? How will acceptance be recorded? Who may approve extra work? What happens if a key person leaves?

Ask qualified counsel to review the legal terms, including ownership, confidentiality, termination and dispute arrangements. Do not assume that a contract used for a different company or country will fit this one.

Give the advisers a usable file

A productive first discussion starts with a short chronology, the current agreement, relevant invoices and a clear explanation of the outcome you want. Preserve the original communications rather than relying entirely on a summary written after a disagreement.

Separate business questions from personal ones. A founder’s Indian property, inheritance or family arrangements may need a different instruction from a commercial contract review.

Finally, agree who coordinates the matter. Having several professionals involved is useful only when their responsibilities and communication are clear.

Separate the company from the founder

A cross-border business discussion can become confused when company assets, personal accounts and family arrangements are treated as one file. Before a consultation, identify which person or entity is a party to each agreement. Record the names as they appear on the documents rather than using a convenient trading name throughout your summary.

If a founder paid an early invoice personally, explain that fact. If a relative owns premises used by the Indian business, explain that too. Your adviser needs to understand the arrangement that exists, including its imperfections. A polished diagram that leaves out awkward details is less useful than an accurate account of how the work and payments actually happen.

Review the points where work changes hands

Cross-border problems often emerge at a handover. A project moves from a salesperson to a delivery team. A supplier begins working with a new contact. A founder assumes that an employee has transferred all files before leaving. Each transition deserves a clear record of what was delivered, what remains outstanding and who can access it.

Make a list of the systems, customer records and business documents that a new team member would need to continue the work. Do not put passwords in that list. Use it to identify ownership and access questions for the appropriate professional. Operational continuity and legal ownership are connected questions, but one does not automatically establish the other.

Prepare for the first disagreement while everyone is cooperating

A disagreement need not begin with dishonesty. One party may believe a task was included in the original price while the other treats it as additional work. One person may regard a message as approval while another considers it a suggestion. These differences are easier to discuss when there is an agreed record.

Decide how changes will be proposed and approved, where the current version of an agreement will be kept and who will reconcile invoices against accepted work. If the parties already disagree, avoid reconstructing a new version of events together. Preserve the records you have and ask counsel how to organise them for review.

Keep professional responsibilities visible

A business setup adviser, accountant and lawyer can all contribute to a cross-border arrangement. The founder should still know which professional is answering which question. An operational recommendation about a licence should not be mistaken for a legal opinion on an Indian contract. An accounting treatment should not be assumed to settle the ownership of an asset.

Ask each adviser to identify the limits of the instruction and the information they need from the others. Keep a short list of unresolved decisions, the professional responsible for each and the date when an answer is needed. This is particularly useful when a transaction is moving quickly and several people are communicating through different channels.

Use a repeatable review routine

Once the immediate issue has been addressed, make the file easier to maintain. Record where signed agreements are stored, who checks renewal dates and how a change of authorised contact will be communicated. Review the arrangement when a shareholder, supplier, business model or market changes.

The purpose is not to create paperwork for its own sake. It is to make future decisions easier to explain. A founder who can provide the current agreement, a reliable chronology and a clear statement of the issue gives professional advisers a stronger starting point than one who has to search through several phones during the first call.

Questions to take into the discussion

Before the meeting, write down the decision you want to make, the deadline you are working towards and the documents you believe support your position. Ask whether anything needs urgent attention before the wider review begins. Then ask for the proposed scope and the next practical step in writing.

A useful consultation should help you understand what is known, what remains uncertain and who needs to act. It should not depend on a promise that every cross-border issue has a simple answer. For a UAE-based founder with Indian connections, that clarity is often the most valuable first result.
